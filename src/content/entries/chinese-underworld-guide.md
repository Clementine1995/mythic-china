## Begin with the word “underworld”

Mythic China uses **underworld** for _yīnjiān_ (<span lang="zh-Hans">阴间</span>), a broad Chinese term for the place entered by the spirits of the dead. The [Ministry of Education dictionary](https://dict.revised.moe.edu.tw/dictView.jsp?ID=156490&q=1&word=%E9%99%B0) defines the term at that general level and lists other names, but a dictionary gloss does not turn those names into one complete religious system.

“Hell” can be useful when a particular source emphasizes punishment. It is too narrow for the collection as a whole. In the model summarized by [Asia for Educators at Columbia University](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/cosmos/prb/underworld.htm), the underworld is temporary rather than a place of eternal punishment for an unchanging self. Judgment, punishment, release, and rebirth belong to a sequence.

That distinction matters. It keeps a familiar English word from silently importing a different theology, and it leaves room for other Chinese accounts of the dead that do not follow the same route.

## One influential route: the Ten Kings

The Columbia teaching resource describes a popular-religious model in which the dead pass through departments governed by ten judges or magistrates. The structure is partly derived from Buddhism and recast through the language of a hierarchy: records are examined, conduct is judged, and officials administer the process.

A Southern Song painting in [The Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/44509) makes that bureaucratic imagination visible. In one scroll from a set of the _Ten Kings of Hell_, a king sits with a scribe and other officials while the dead receive judgment and demons carry out punishments below. The museum dates the work to before 1195 and identifies it as one of five surviving scrolls from an original set of ten.

The painting is evidence for a particular Buddhist visual tradition, not a floor plan of a universally accepted afterlife. Even within Ten Kings imagery, the order, timing, figures, and punishments require object- or text-specific support.

## Judgment is part of a passage

In this Ten Kings model, the courts do not mark the end of the story. The Met describes a sequence of hearings leading toward transmigration into another life. Columbia’s account likewise connects the final court to reincarnation and stresses that punishment is not eternal.

Ritual links the living to that passage. Columbia explains that mourning and funerary rites were performed with the welfare of the deceased in mind, including the hope of helping a soul move through the courts. The exact rite, specialist, offering, and regional practice must be researched separately; this guide does not treat one outline as a script for all Chinese funerals.

## A reading rule for this collection

Each Entry in _The Chinese Underworld_ should answer four questions before it makes a broad claim:

- Which period and region does the evidence describe?
- Is the evidence a text, an artwork, a ritual record, or a later interpretation?
- Does a named court, deity, punishment, or route belong to one source or to a wider documented pattern?
- Is an English term a neutral gloss, or does it introduce assumptions the Chinese source does not make?

This is also why Zhong Kui appears in the collection as a featured protector, not as its ruler. Museum records identify him as a demon queller and connect his image with protective occasions, but the present evidence does not make him one of the Ten Kings or a universal underworld official. The collection places him near the threshold as an editorial reading path; it does not convert that placement into religious fact.
