## Advice and a warning before the disguise is seen

The [Shanghai Library's Qingketing text](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E8%81%8A%E6%96%8B%E5%BF%97%E5%BC%82.%E5%8D%81%E5%85%AD%E5%8D%B7.%E6%B8%85.%E8%92%B2%E6%9D%BE%E9%BE%84.%E6%92%B0.%E6%B8%85%E4%B9%BE%E9%9A%86%E4%B8%89%E5%8D%81%E4%B8%80%E5%B9%B4%E9%9D%92%E6%9F%AF%E4%BA%AD%E5%88%8A%E6%9C%AC.pdf) begins Painted Skin on digital page 72. Wang secretly shelters a stranger who appears to be a young woman. When his wife later advises him to send her away, he continues to shelter her. On page 73, a Taoist warns that an evil presence is clinging to him. Wang briefly suspects the stranger, then reassures himself that she is plainly beautiful and dismisses the Taoist as a fraud.

These events come from one version of the tale. They are not evidence about Qing marriage or religious practice, and the Taoist's warning does not identify the figure.

## A skin that can be put on

On digital page 73, Wang looks through a window and sees a hideous being spread a human skin on a couch, paint it, lift it as if shaking out a garment, put it on, and become a woman.

> “It spread a human skin on the couch, took up a brush, and painted it.”

The quoted line is our translation of a short, unpunctuated passage in the 1766 Qingketing text (digital page 73). The wording presents the beautiful appearance as something painted and wearable. It does not identify the figure as a fox or add visual details beyond the actions described here.

## What this version calls the figure

When the Taoist confronts the figure on digital page 74, the text uses **yè mèi** as a rebuke, rendered here as **“wicked apparition.”** It also uses several expressions containing the word for ghost, but it does not call the figure a fox in this version of Painted Skin.

Here, “wicked apparition” preserves the force of the rebuke without treating it as a fixed species name. Other textual witnesses may differ; this article does not explain when or why.

## The cost of recognition

Across digital pages 73–75, the figure tears open Wang's abdomen and takes his heart. Wang's wife seeks help first from the Taoist and then from a beggar. The beggar insults and strikes her, spits a handful of phlegm, holds it to her lips, and orders her to swallow it. She forces it down. Later, while she tends Wang's body, a mass surges out and falls into his open body cavity. Only then does she see that it is a human heart. She binds the opening; the tale says Wang is breathing at midnight and alive by dawn.

The tale makes Wang's wife endure the degrading treatment that precedes his recovery. It is narrated action, not a medical claim. The text then shifts from story to closing commentary. That commentary rebukes mistaking the monstrous for beauty and sincerity for falsehood, and connects coveting another's beauty with the wife's forced swallowing of the beggar's spittle.

## A modern scholar's reading

[Wing Bo Anna Tso](https://doi.org/10.4312/ala.7.2.9-18) argues that Painted Skin can be read as a parody of the imitative structures of gender (pages 15–18). She also argues that Herbert Giles's 1880 translation suppresses elements she regards as transgressive. This is Tso's interpretation; it does not identify the figure in the 1766 text or establish the tale's only meaning.

This article follows the Shanghai Library's 1766 Qingketing copy on Commons, digital pages 72–75. It reports Tso's published comparison with Giles but does not independently compare modern translations or films; those questions require separate edition- and scene-level evidence.
